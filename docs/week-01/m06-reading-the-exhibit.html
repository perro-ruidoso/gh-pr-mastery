GitHub Pull Requests for Agentic Coders Week 1 · Objective M06

Reading the Exhibit

A repository records how its work was done. Learn to read that record and you can audit any team's process — including your own — from the outside, without asking anyone.

M06 · Evidence Bloom: Analyze Assessed by: A1 (written)

Objective: Read the course repo's own issue graph and PR history as an exhibit and describe the workflow that produced it.

Why this page exists

Everything else this week taught you a mechanism. This one teaches you to infer process from artifacts — and it is the skill that makes the other five compound. A merged pull request is not just a change that landed; it is a record of what someone decided, in what order, with what checks, and who agreed.

You need this for three reasons. You will join codebases whose conventions nobody has written down. You will be graded, in this course, on the evidence your repository leaves rather than on your account of what you did. And from Week 2 onward you will be directing an agent whose work you can only supervise through exactly these traces.

closes: [14404]The PR body used a linking keyword. The issue and the change are formally bound, not just mentioned in prose.

The method

Four questions, in this order. They work on any repository.

1. What is the unit of work?

Does one PR correspond to one issue, or do PRs arrive unlinked? Count how many merged PRs close an issue:

$ gh pr list -R OWNER/REPO --state merged --limit 30 \
    --json number,title,closingIssuesReferences \
    --jq '[.[] | select(.closingIssuesReferences|length > 0)] | length'

A high ratio means work is specified before it is done. A low one means the issue tracker is decoration.

2. How big are the changes?

$ gh pr list -R OWNER/REPO --state merged --limit 30 \
    --json number,additions,deletions \
    --jq 'sort_by(-(.additions + .deletions)) | .[0:5] | .[] | "\(.number)\t\(.additions + .deletions)"'

The distribution tells you whether review is real. A repository whose median PR is 1,200 lines is not reviewing; it is approving.

3. Who contributes, and from where?

isCrossRepository separates maintainers from outside contributors. A repo with no fork PRs has a closed contribution model; one with many has a triage burden, which is exactly what Week 6 puts you in the seat for.

4. What gates did it pass?

$ gh pr view NNN -R OWNER/REPO --json reviewDecision,statusCheckRollup,mergedBy

Was it approved, and by someone other than the author? Did checks pass before the merge or after? Week 5 is about configuring these; today you only need to read them.

Infer carefully — absence of evidence is not evidence

A PR with no review comments may mean the change was trivial, or that it was discussed in Slack, or that review is a formality here. You can say "there is no recorded review discussion on this PR" with certainty. You cannot say "this change was not reviewed." Keep your claims to what the artifacts actually support — and say which artifact supports each one. That distinction is most of the A1 rubric.

Your target: this course's own repository

The repository holding the pages you are reading was built using the workflow this course teaches. Each of its six units was filed as a spec issue, decomposed into tickets with explicit blocking edges, built on a branch, reviewed, and merged. That was done deliberately so you would have a real, honest example to read in Week 1 — including the parts that went sideways.

Your assignment target

Pick one merged pull request in perro-ruidoso/gh-pr-mastery and write roughly 300 words answering: what did it change, what was it blocked by, and how can you tell? Every claim must name the artifact that supports it — a field in --json output, a linked issue number, a timestamp, a review thread.

Some things you might notice: which issues have blocking edges declared; whether a PR's base was main or another feature branch; whether any issue was closed by hand rather than by a merge; how long the gap was between a PR opening and its first review.

You may find decisions in there you disagree with. Say so — a well-argued criticism of the course repository's own process earns full marks, provided it is grounded in what the artifacts show.

Hands-on checklist

Self-check

1. A PR merged at 15:55:40 and its linked issue closed at 15:55:42. What does the two-second gap tell you?

B. Two seconds is machine time, and closingIssuesReferences on the PR confirms the formal link. A human closing an issue deliberately leaves a much larger and more variable gap. (D is ruled out by the order of the timestamps.)

2. Which claim is fully supported by "isCrossRepository": false?

D. That is exactly and only what the field means. Membership, review, and contribution policy are separate questions answered by other fields — author, reviewDecision, and the repository's settings. Over-reading a single field is the most common failure in this kind of analysis.

3. You find a merged PR with no review comments. What is the defensible statement?

C. Report the absence, not a motive. The repository records what happened in the repository; conversations in chat, pairing sessions, and hallway reviews leave no trace. Note also that a PR can carry a formal approval with no comments at all — check reviewDecision before concluding anything.

4. Why does a repository's PR-size distribution tell you something about its review culture?

A. Size is a proxy for reviewability, and reviewability is a precondition for review meaning anything. (B is a real UI behaviour but not the point; C is unsupportable — a mechanical rename can be enormous and trivial, which is why you read size alongside what changed; D is true and irrelevant.)
