Severity Words and the Comments That Force a Question

The course uses three severity words and one honest fourth. This page fixes their meanings, maps them onto the verdict that ends a review, and catalogues the five comment shapes that make an author reply with a question instead of a commit — with the rewrite for each. It closes with the one-paragraph template Assignment A4's rubric grades against.

M22 · Communication Bloom: Create Assessed by: A4 paired (rubric)

Objective: Write review comments a peer can act on without asking a clarifying question: located, specific, and severity-marked.

Three words, one honest fourth

First wordMeaningWhat the author owesEffect on your verdict
must-fixThe PR should not merge as it stands. A rule is broken (quote it) or behaviour is wrong (show the value).A fix, or an argument that the rule does not apply — in the thread, before resolving it.At least one open must-fix → REQUEST_CHANGES.
nitReal, worth doing, not blocking. Naming, a stale comment, a clearer test name.Fix it or decline it with a sentence; either is fine.Nits alone → APPROVE (with the nits) or COMMENT.
questionYou genuinely do not know, and the answer changes your verdict. Say what you would conclude each way.An answer in the thread.Hold the verdict at COMMENT until answered — or ask before you review.
praiseSomething worth keeping doing. Located too — on the line.Nothing.None; but Google's guide is right that "people learn from reinforcement of what they are doing well and not just what they could do better."

The first three are the course's verdict vocabulary from M21 (CONTEXT.md: "must-fix, nit, or false positive") applied at the comment level — a false positive never becomes a comment, because a finding you have rejected is not the author's problem. Google's labels are the same idea with different words: "Nit: This is a minor thing … Optional (or Consider): I think this may be a good idea, but it's not strictly required … FYI: I don't expect you to do this in this CL". Pick one vocabulary per team and use it as the first word, every time; the point is that the author can sort the threads without reading them.

A question is a comment, not a disguise

"Are you sure SM-2 resets EF here?" (page 1's anti-example) is not a question. The reviewer knows the answer and is making the author say it. A real question names what you would conclude either way: "question — does Card.__post_init__ run when replace() builds the new card? If yes, this floor bug raises instead of storing 1.16 and the test should expect ValueError; if no, it stores a bad value and the store will reject it later." That is answerable in one word, and both answers lead somewhere.

Five shapes that force a question

ShapeExampleThe question it forcesRewrite
The floating verdict — a Conversation comment about code"The ease factor logic doesn't match the ADR.""Which line?"Move it onto line 45 as an inline comment; quote the ADR sentence.
The unsourced "wrong""This rounding is wrong.""According to what? What does it produce?""ADR 0001, Rounding: 'Do not reach for the built-in round()…' — interval 5 × ease 2.5 gives 12, the ADR gives 13."
The hedge — "I think", "might", "maybe""I think the floor might be in the wrong place?""Is this blocking? Did you check?"Check, then either "must-fix — …" or delete the comment. A hedge is a verdict you have not finished.
The diagnosis without a prescription"This breaks the failure rule.""So what should it be?"Add the fix: the suggestion block on line 50, or "keep card.ease_factor" in words.
The unmarked pile — five comments, no severitiesFive paragraphs, one of them a typo, one a wrong value"Which of these do I have to do before you'll approve?"First word on each; and the summary body says how many are must-fix.

A sixth shape belongs to the author, not the reviewer, and M23 covers it: the reply that says "done" without a commit SHA. The two are mirror images — a comment the author cannot act on, and a reply the reviewer cannot verify.

Courtesy is a property of the sentence

Google's guide: "be sure that you are always making comments about the code and never making comments about the developer." Its own pair — bad: "Why did you use threads here when there's obviously no benefit to be gained from concurrency?"; good: "The concurrency model here is adding complexity to the system without any actual performance benefit that I can see." — shows the mechanism: the subject of the good sentence is the code. Every comment on PR #15 has a rule or a value as its subject ("This clamps before the update", "this assertion was 2.36 before this PR"); the one place it addresses the author — "The SM-2 parenthetical you quote" — it is about what they quoted, not about them. Severity words help here too: nit in front of a naming complaint tells the author it is small before they can read it as a slight.

The template

One inline comment — the shape A4's rubric grades

<must-fix | nit | question> — <source>: "<the sentence from the ADR, CONTEXT.md, or the ticket>." <What the code does instead, with a concrete value, exception, or failing test.> <The change: a ```suggestion block if it is one contiguous edit; otherwise one imperative sentence naming the lines.>

And the summary body that carries the verdict: how many must-fix, how many nits, and one sentence on what would change your verdict. PR #15's: "Three must-fix items, each a rule the ADR states in so many words, and one nit." The author read that line and knew the shape of their afternoon.

Hands-on checklist

Assignment A4, item 3 — before you submit the batch on your peer's PR:

Self-check

1. A review has four inline comments marked nit and none marked must-fix. Which verdict fits?

C. The marks and the verdict must agree: a nit is by definition not blocking. A contradicts the marks; B invents a rule; D leaves the author with no signal, which is the unmarked pile in another form.

2. "I think the floor might be applied in the wrong place?" — which shape is this, and what is the fix?

A. "I think … might … ?" is a verdict the reviewer has not finished; the author's only reply is "did you check?". It is not a real question (C) because it does not say what each answer would mean. Its location is not the problem (B); D misreads it.

3. Why does a finding you classified as a false positive in M21 never become a review comment?

D. Verdicts filter findings; only must-fix and nit survive into comments. A false positive goes in your write-up (A4's written part), not on the PR. A is false; B and C invent obligations.

Flashcards

Say the answer out loud before you flip. "Again" puts the card back in the queue; "Got it" retires it. Keyboard: Space flips, 1 again, 2 got it.

The four first words, and which one is not a verdict?
must-fix, nit, question, praise — question and praise carry no verdict; a false positive never becomes a comment at all.
Marks → verdict?
Any open must-fix → REQUEST_CHANGES; nits only → APPROVE or COMMENT; an unanswered real question → hold at COMMENT.
What makes a question real rather than a disguised verdict?
You do not know the answer, and you say what you would conclude for each answer.
The five shapes that force a question?
Floating verdict (not on a line), unsourced "wrong", the hedge, diagnosis without prescription, the unmarked pile.
Google's severity labels?
"Nit", "Optional (or Consider)", "FYI" — "This makes review intent explicit and helps authors prioritize."
Google's courtesy rule, in one sentence?
"Always making comments about the code and never making comments about the developer."
The comment template?
First word — source: "quoted rule." What the code does instead (value/exception/test). The change (suggestion block or one imperative sentence).
What does the summary body carry?
The counts (how many must-fix, how many nits) and what would change the verdict. PR #15: "Three must-fix items … and one nit."
Ask your teacher

Take one comment you marked nit and one you marked must-fix and ask Claude Code to argue each should be the other. If it can move a must-fix to nit without a rule going unbroken, your source was weak; if it can promote a nit, you undercounted. Bring the pair whose severity you still cannot settle to the weekly meeting — that is a genuine question, and it belongs in the room, not on the PR.
